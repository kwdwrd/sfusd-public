\begin{abstract}
Popular media suggests that school lotteries are unpopular with families. We leverage San Francisco Unified School District's (SFUSD) transitional kindergarten (TK) promotion priority---under which the 2024--25 TK cohort was offered a guaranteed kindergarten seat at its own school for 2025--26, in exchange for leaving the kindergarten lottery---to quantify families' distaste for lottery participation. Counting promoted families as having ranked their own school first, schools with TK programs received relatively more first-choice requests than schools without; observed first-choice requests, which exclude those families, instead fell by \numDidFirstTwoPct. Building on a revealed-preference model of school value, we show that distaste for lottery participation is roughly equivalent to the quality difference between the best and median schools in the district. Because aggregate data cannot separate this distaste from selection on match value, we read the estimate as an upper bound, and set out what individual-level choice data would identify.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Assignment lotteries are a common mechanism for allocating public school seats: as of 2022, 71 of the 300 largest school districts in the United States used a centralized-admission lottery (\cite{lagosfranciscoEffectCentralizedAdmissionSchool} and references therein). While assignment lotteries are believed to have a salutary effect on district enrollment, the popular conception is that families dislike them. For example, changes to Chile's \textit{Sistema de Admisi\'{o}n Escolar} were covered with a newspaper illustration of a child strapped to a roulette wheel~\cite{cabalinEducacionPortadaVisualizacion2016}; and in SFUSD's own fall 2026 survey of families, 82\% chose a guaranteed spot near home when asked to pick between predictability and choice.\footnote{SFUSD, \emph{Fall 2026 Family Survey and Focus Groups on SFUSD's Student Assignment Policy}, \url{https://www.sfusd.edu/school-reorganization/fall-2026-family-survey-and-focus-groups-sfusds-student-assignment-policy}. The survey drew 2{,}978 responses (1{,}871 SFUSD families, 1{,}107 not in SFUSD) between 11 September and 9 October 2026. It is not a random sample, and respondents skew White and higher-income relative to SFUSD families; the district reports that reweighting to neighborhood and income moves this figure by no more than three points.}

Apparent distaste for assignment lotteries has combined with the ambiguous ability of lotteries to achieve their stated aims of improving student academic outcomes and decreasing segregation to yield a policy environment in which assignment lotteries are a target for de-implementation (typically in favor of traditional neighborhood schooling). If assignment lotteries are fulfilling no social objectives, and are scaring families away from public schools, then it stands to reason that they should be eliminated. In San Francisco, for example, school funding is determined by measured enrollment. A mechanism change that reduced San Francisco's private schooling rate from \numPrivateShare{} to the national average of \numNationalPrivateShare{} would increase the system's LCFF revenue by roughly \numLcffGain{} annually.\footnote{Own calculation; the chain is in \texttt{lcff\_counterfactual.py} and \texttt{output/lcff\_counterfactual.csv}. SFUSD enrolls \numSfusdEnrollment{} students in its own (non-charter) schools, and San Francisco's private schools about \numPrivateStudents, or \numPrivateShare{} of the city's K--12 students; the national private share is \numNationalPrivateShare{} (NCES). Moving to that share returns \numStudentsGained{} students to the district---\numAdaGained{} in average daily attendance, after SFUSD's attendance ratio, and after allocating the gain between the district and charter schools in current proportions. Under the Local Control Funding Formula these students add the base grant, but, not being unduplicated pupils, they dilute the concentration grant SFUSD draws because its unduplicated pupil percentage (\numUPP) exceeds the \numUPPthreshold{} threshold: an influx this size takes it to \numUPPafter, below the threshold, so the concentration grant is lost on existing students too. Hence \numLcffGain{} rather than the \numLcffNaiveGain{} that SFUSD's current \numLcffPerAda{} per ADA would imply. The same formula reproduces SFUSD's reported LCFF per ADA to within \numLcffModelError, which is the check on it. This is revenue, not net of the cost of educating the additional students.}

To address the idea that a change to the school assignment system may improve enrollment, it is necessary to understand families' distaste for lottery participation. San Francisco's recent changes to its assignment lottery priorities provide a natural context in which to evaluate the magnitude of this distaste. San Francisco's school lottery is run as deferred acceptance (to a first approximation): students apply to schools in rank order, and each school has a priority order over students. The district computes a market priority for each school such that students who applied and are above this priority are admitted, and other students are rejected (cite the simple descriptions paper). The district's priority classes are coarse. In the 2023--24 school year SFUSD expanded its transitional kindergarten offerings and, to encourage enrollment, offered TK students top priority for kindergarten enrollment at their TK's associated elementary school. As a practical matter, all TK students who wished to remain at their school were allowed to do so.

At the same time, schools in SFUSD are commonly known to have heterogeneous qualities, based on parent beliefs. This, together with the fact that not all kindergarten sites have associated TK programs, suggests an empirical test. TK seats are scarce---there are not enough for every family that wants one---so TK enrollment is itself the outcome of a choice, a point we return to below. On the reading we test, a family that remains at a ``low-quality'' elementary school after TK reveals a distaste for lottery participation large enough to outweigh the quality it gives up, while a family that leaves a ``high-quality'' elementary school for the lottery reveals the opposite. Both readings require that TK families value their school as the city does. Section~\ref{sec:promotion-rates} shows they need not, and that aggregate data cannot separate distaste for the lottery from a taste for one's own school; that limitation is the central argument of this paper. By comparing the lottery participation rate of TK families to the measured quality of their associated elementary school, we can estimate the magnitude of families' distaste for lottery participation.

We first show that the TK promotion policy sharply reduced the number of families ranking kindergartens with TK programs first, and that counting promoted families as having ranked their own school first more than offsets that fall. This is consistent with both selection into TK programs and a distaste for lottery participation. We then estimate a revealed-preference model of school quality.\footnote{The approach follows \cite{aryal2025}, who show that a collective ranking constructed from submitted rank-order lists converges to the true quality ranking as the market grows large.} Next, we build a model of lottery participation in which a family may accept its current school or enter a costly lottery over all schools. We find that the cost of the lottery is roughly equivalent to the quality difference between the best and median schools in SFUSD. Because the same take-up pattern is equally consistent with families valuing their own school above the citywide average, we read this as an upper bound on distaste, and devote \S\ref{sec:individual-data} to what individual-level choice data would identify.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}
\label{section: data}

SFUSD provides aggregated ranking data for its annual assignment lottery. While the lottery places students for any school year, the main participation points (cite) are kindergarten, 6th grade, and 9th grade. We focus solely on kindergarten applications, as these applications constitute a large and independent market.

The lottery data are available for 15 application cycles, covering applications for the 2012--13 academic year through applications for the 2026--27 academic year. For each cycle, the data report the number of families who ranked each kindergarten program at each rank, and also the total number of families who applied. Ranks are reported individually only for the first five to nine choices, with deeper ranks pooled into a top-coded bucket, and the scheme changes across cycles: 2012--13 through 2018--19 report ranks 1--7 and then eighth-or-deeper; 2019--20 and 2020--21 report ranks 1--5, 6--10, and two deeper buckets; 2022--23 onward report ranks 1--9 and then tenth-or-deeper; and 2021--22 carries both schemes. List lengths are therefore variable and observed only coarsely beyond the ninth rank. The data also report the number of families who accepted automatic promotion from TK to kindergarten at each TK program in 2025--26.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}
We apply a random-utility (logit) model of school preferences, written at the level of the individual family so that it can be taken directly to choice-level data (rank-order lists, home locations, TK enrollment and promotion decisions) once we obtain them via public records request. With only school-by-year request counts in hand today, every family-level term below is empty and the model collapses to a school fixed effect; we write it out in full so that the aggregate estimates in this draft are visibly a special case of the model we eventually want to estimate.

\subsection{Preferences}
Index families by $i$, kindergarten programs by $k \in \mathcal{K}_t$, and application cycles by $t$. Family $i$'s utility from attending $k$ is
\[
u_{ikt} = \underbrace{\delta_k}_{\text{school FE}} \; + \; \underbrace{x_i'\beta_k}_{\substack{\text{student} \\ \text{characteristics}}} \; + \; \underbrace{z_{ik}'\gamma}_{\substack{\text{(student, school)} \\ \text{characteristics}}} \; + \; \varepsilon_{ikt},
\]
where
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\delta_k$ is the school fixed effect: the common component of every family's valuation of $k$ (what the rest of this draft calls $\theta_k$);
    \item $x_i$ are exogenous family characteristics (race/ethnicity, home language and EL status, FRPM eligibility, CTIP1 status, TK enrollment). Because $x_i$ does not vary across schools, it enters only through school-specific (or school-attribute-specific) slopes $\beta_k$---e.g.\ $x_i'\beta_k = \sum_m x_i' B\, w_{km}$ for observed school attributes $w_{km}$ (immersion language, test scores, demographic composition)---so that, e.g., Spanish-speaking families can value Spanish immersion differently;
    \item $z_{ik}$ are exogenous (family, school) characteristics: commute distance from home to $k$, whether $k$ is $i$'s attendance-area school, whether $i$ has an older sibling enrolled at $k$, whether $k$ is $i$'s TK site. Distance is the term that will eventually let us convert utility into dollars;
    \item $\varepsilon_{ikt}$ is i.i.d.\ Type-I extreme value with unit scale, which normalizes the units of every other term.
\end{itemize}
The outside option---not applying to a school, which includes private school, charter, leaving San Francisco, or simply ranking fewer schools---has utility
\[
u_{i0t} = \xi_t + x_i'\beta_0 + \varepsilon_{i0t},
\]
where $\xi_t$ is a year effect absorbing citywide shifts in the attractiveness of SFUSD (e.g.\ the post-2020 enrollment decline). Only differences in utility are identified, so we normalize $\beta_0 = 0$ and one year's $\xi_t$ to zero.

\subsection{Applications}
SFUSD's assignment mechanism is a variant of deferred acceptance, which is strategy-proof for families when rank lists are unrestricted, so we take a family's submitted rank list to be its true preference order over the schools it finds acceptable. Family $i$ ranks $k$ if and only if $u_{ikt} \ge u_{i0t}$, and orders the ranked schools by $u_{ikt}$. The likelihood of an observed list $k_1 \succ k_2 \succ \cdots \succ k_{L} \succ 0$ is the rank-ordered (``exploded'') logit,
\[
\Pr\left( k_1 \succ \cdots \succ k_L \succ 0 \right) = \prod_{\ell=1}^{L} \frac{\exp v_{ik_\ell t}}{\exp v_{i0t} + \sum_{m \ge \ell} \exp v_{ik_m t} + \sum_{j \notin \{k_1,\dots,k_L\}} \exp v_{ijt}} \times \frac{\exp v_{i0t}}{\exp v_{i0t} + \sum_{j \notin \{k_1,\dots,k_L\}} \exp v_{ijt}},
\]
with $v_{ikt} = u_{ikt} - \varepsilon_{ikt}$ the deterministic part. With individual lists, $(\delta, \beta, \gamma, \xi)$ are estimated by maximum likelihood.

\subsection{TK automatic promotion}
A family $i$ whose child attended TK at school $k(i)$ in 2024--25 was offered a guaranteed kindergarten seat at $k(i)$, conditional on not entering the lottery. Accepting yields
\[
U^{\text{accept}}_{i} = v_{i k(i) t} + c + \eta_{i1},
\]
while declining and entering the lottery yields the expected value of the lottery assignment,
\[
U^{\text{lottery}}_{i} = \underbrace{\sum_{\ell} v_{ik_\ell t} \, P_{ik_\ell} \prod_{m < \ell}\left( 1 - P_{ik_m} \right)}_{\equiv\, \mathrm{EU}_i} + \eta_{i0},
\]
where $P_{ik}$ is $i$'s admission probability at $k$ (a function of SFUSD's priority categories, which are themselves functions of $z_{ik}$ and $x_i$), the sum runs over $i$'s rank list, and $\eta$ are i.i.d.\ Type-I extreme value. The parameter of interest is $c$: the utility a family will give up to avoid the lottery, i.e.\ its distaste for lottery participation (equivalently, a certainty premium). Then
\[
\Pr\left( \text{accept}_i \right) = \Lambda\left( v_{ik(i)t} - \mathrm{EU}_i + c \right),
\]
with $\Lambda$ the logistic CDF. Because the acceptance decision is a binary logit with the same utility index, $c$ is measured in the same units as $\delta_k$, and (once $\gamma_{\text{distance}}$ is estimated) in dollars.

\subsection{What survives without choice data}
\label{sec:individual-data}
With only aggregate counts we must set $\beta = 0$ and $\gamma = 0$. Then $v_{ikt} = \delta_k$, $v_{i0t} = \xi_t$, every family is identical up to $\varepsilon$, and the probability any given applicant ranks $k$ is $\exp\delta_k / (\exp\delta_k + \exp\xi_t)$.\footnote{This treats ``rank $k$ vs.\ not'' as a binary comparison of $k$ with the outside option, which is what the rank-ordered logit implies for the marginal event $\{u_{ik} \ge u_{i0}\}$.} Writing $\theta_k \equiv \delta_k$ and $\theta_{0t} \equiv \xi_t$ to match the rest of the draft, the number of applications to school $k$ is
\[
A_{kt} = N_t \times \frac{\exp \theta_k}{\exp\theta_k + \exp\theta_{0t}} \;\; \implies \;\; \ln A_{kt} - \ln\left[ N_t - A_{kt} \right] = \theta_k - \theta_{0t},
\]
which we estimate by OLS of the log-odds $\ln A_{kt} - \ln(N_t - A_{kt})$ on school and year fixed effects. (The log-odds are not well approximated by the log-share $\ln A_{kt} - \ln N_t$: the most popular schools draw up to half of a year's applicants, e.g.\ Clarendon ES in 2017--18.) Wherever 2025--26 data are used, both $A_{kt}$ and $N_t$ add back the families who accepted automatic promotion, reconstructing applications as if the opt-out did not exist.

\paragraph{Estimation window.} Our lottery panel covers 15 application cycles, 2012--13 through 2026--27, but the main specification estimates $\theta_k$ from the four cycles 2021--22 through 2024--25 only. These are the cycles families deciding on promotion (in early 2025) had observed; they exclude the post-decision 2026--27 cycle and the treatment year itself; and their request volume matches the CDE enrollment years (2023--24, 2024--25) we use for seat capacity, whereas the 2015--18 cycles saw more than twice today's requests (about 50{,}000 ranked requests a year against about 20{,}000 now). This window covers \numSchools{} kindergarten programs. Section~\ref{sec:quality-over-time} shows the results are insensitive to the window and to measuring quality year by year.

\paragraph{Schools versus programs (a data limitation).} The lottery reports requests by program, and the promotion roster lists eligibility and acceptance by TK program, but CDE reports kindergarten enrollment (our capacity measure) only by school. We therefore estimate quality, admission probabilities, and promotion rates at the school (building) level. Two promotion-eligible buildings run two TK programs each---Chinese Immersion School at DeAvila (two Chinese-immersion strands) and Dolores Huerta (two Spanish strands)---so we sum their roster rows to the building before merging with the lottery panel, and both strands share one $\theta_k$ and one admission probability. This merge is forced by the capacity data rather than a modeling choice: if demand differs between the strands of a building, we cannot see it. (An earlier version of the pipeline merged the roster program by program, which double-counted these two schools in every year of the panel; fixing this moved no reported estimate by more than $0.01$.) Similarly, admission probabilities become school-level, $P_{ik} = P_k$, $\mathrm{EU}_i = \mathrm{EU}$ for every family, and the promotion decision becomes
\[
\Pr\left( \text{accept} \mid k \right) = \Lambda\left( \theta_k - \mathrm{EU} + c \right),
\]
which we take to the school-level promotion counts in \S\ref{sec:promotion-rates}. Everything the individual-level data add enters through $x_i$, $z_{ik}$, and individual $P_{ik}$; in particular, $z_{ik}$ (distance, attendance area, sibling) is what lets the model distinguish ``this family prefers its TK school'' from ``this family dislikes the lottery,'' which is the main selection concern below.

\textbf{Observation.} The number of applicants $N_t$ is endogenous to the outside option $\theta_{0t}$. However, the average applicant applies to about \numReqPerApplicant{} schools; IIA in the logit model implies that, empirically, $N_t$ should be effectively independent of $\theta_{0t}$.

\paragraph{Data requested.} For each kindergarten applicant (and each 2024--25 TK student) in 2012--13 through 2026--27, at the program level: an anonymized ID; full submitted rank list; home census block or attendance area; CTIP1 status; sibling priority by school; TK school and promotion offer/decision; assignment outcome; and October enrollment (to see who left SFUSD). Optionally: race/ethnicity, home language, FRPM status. Also: 2023--24 TK applications and rank lists, since where a 2024--25 TK family ranked its TK school reveals its own taste for that school; and kindergarten seat capacity by program.

TK-promotion eligibility appears roughly unrelated to measured school quality.

\subsection{Effect of promotion on applications}
\label{sec:application-effects}
\paragraph{A mechanical model.} Let $N_t$ be the number of lottery applicants in cycle $t$, $P_{kt}$ the number of families who accepted promotion at TK school $k$, and $P_t = \sum_k P_{kt}$, so that absent promotion the cohort would have been $N_t + P_t$ families. A general applicant ranks school $k$ with probability $s_{kt}$ (under the logit model of \S\ref{sec:model}, $s_{kt} = \exp\theta_k / (\exp\theta_k + \exp\theta_{0t})$). A promoted family from school $j$ would, absent promotion, have ranked its own school with probability $q$ and any other school $k$ with the general probability $s_{kt}$. Then observed applications to $k$ are $A_{kt} = N_t s_{kt}$, while the but-for count is
\[
A^{\mathrm{cf}}_{kt} = (N_t + P_t)\, s_{kt} + P_{kt}\,(q - s_{kt}),
\]
and promotion changes applications by
\[
\ln A_{kt} - \ln A^{\mathrm{cf}}_{kt} = \underbrace{-\ln\left( 1 + \frac{P_t}{N_t} \right)}_{\text{every school}} \; \underbrace{- \ln\left( 1 + \frac{P_{kt}\,(q - s_{kt})}{(N_t + P_t)\, s_{kt}} \right)}_{\text{TK schools only}} \;\approx\; -\frac{P_t}{N_t + P_t} - q\,\frac{P_{kt}}{A^{\mathrm{cf}}_{kt}} .
\]
The first term is common to all schools: removing \numPromoted{} families from a but-for cohort of \numCohort{} lowers applications everywhere by $\ln(\numCohort/\numApplicantsTreat) = \numCommonEffect$ log points. The second term is the extra loss at a TK school: roughly the share of the school's but-for applications that its own promoted families would have supplied, scaled by $q$. The same algebra applies to first-choice requests, with $q$ replaced by $f$, the probability a promoted family would have ranked its own school \emph{first}. Because promoted families are a small share of any school's total requests but a large share of its first choices, the model predicts a much larger effect on first choices: under $q = f = 1$, the TK-specific effect averages $\numPredAllQone$ log points for all ranked requests and $\numPredFirstQone$ for first choices across the \numEligibleBuildings{} eligible schools. A difference-in-differences comparison of eligible and other schools removes the common term (absorbed by cycle effects) and estimates the TK-specific one; inverting it gives the implied $q$ or $f$.

The model assumes promoted families would otherwise have behaved like other applicants apart from their attachment to their own school, and that promotion did not change how remaining families applied; the second is consistent with a strategy-proof mechanism, in which rank lists should not respond to how many seats others have claimed, but need not hold in practice.

Right: event-study coefficients on eligible $\times$ cycle (log points, 2024--25 $= 0$) for first-choice and all ranked requests, with 95\% confidence intervals clustered by school. \paragraph{Results.} Promotion sharply reduced first-choice demand at eligible schools. The simple comparison of 2024--25 and 2025--26 gives an effect of $\numDidFirstTwo$ log points (about \numDidFirstTwoPct), implying $f \approx \numImpliedFTwo$: most promoted families would have ranked their own TK school first. The pooled specification (2) gives a smaller effect, but the event study shows why: first choices at eligible schools were rising relative to other schools in every pre-period cycle since 2020--21 (Figure~\ref{fig:application-effects}, left: flat at eligible schools while falling elsewhere), so averaging over the pre-period understates the break. One explanation consistent with the model is that growing TK classes were already supplying first choices to their own schools before promotion made applying unnecessary. Allowing a linear pre-trend for eligible schools (3) gives $\numDidFirstTrend$ log points and $f \approx \numImpliedFTrend$. PPML estimates are similar. We maintain $f = 1$ throughout: a family that accepts a guaranteed seat at its own school would have ranked that school first had it applied. Figure~\ref{figure: first-rankings by school cohort} is the direct evidence---adding the promoted families back more than accounts for the fall in first-choice requests at eligible schools. The difference-in-differences estimates above imply somewhat less, and we read that gap as reflecting the pre-trend the event study shows rather than as evidence that promoted families would have ranked elsewhere. We distinguish two objects. Whether a family would have \emph{ranked its own school first} is not the same as whether it \emph{prefers that school to the lottery}: accepting promotion reveals the second directly, and implies the first only because a family holding top priority that ranks its school first is assured of a seat there, which makes ranking first and accepting equivalent choices in this setting. That equivalence would fail if submitted lists were length-constrained or if priority were uncertain. The distinction has teeth here, because the sign of the first-choice comparison depends on it: adding promoted families back raises eligible schools' first-choice count relative to other schools only if $f$ exceeds \numFBreakeven, and every $f$ the difference-in-differences implies is below that. The reversal reported above therefore rests on the maintained $f = 1$, not on the estimated $f$. The effect persists into 2026--27, consistent with promotion continuing.

Effects on all ranked requests are smaller and imprecise ($\numDidAllMin$ to $\numDidAllMax$ log points, none significant at 5\% by OLS), as the model predicts: promoted families were a tenth of an eligible school's total requests, so even $q = 1$ implies only a $\numPredAllQoneAbs$ log-point TK-specific drop. The implied $q$ ranges from \numImpliedQMin{} to \numImpliedQMax{} across specifications, with confidence intervals spanning both zero and one. Column (4) offers a sharper test of the model's cross-school prediction, regressing on each school's model-predicted effect: schools where promotion should have mattered more did lose more. For first choices the coefficient is $\numIntensityFirst$ (OLS; $\numIntensityFirstPPML$ PPML), of the same order as the implied $f$ from columns (1)--(3); its confidence interval includes the $1$ that $f = 1$ predicts, as does that of the all-ranks coefficient below. For all ranked requests it is $\numIntensityAll$ (s.e.\ $\numIntensityAllSE$), above one: high-intensity schools lost more total requests than their promoted families alone account for. That would be consistent with remaining families ranking schools less often when promotion had visibly claimed many of their seats, but the estimate is imprecise and the confidence interval includes one.

We now turn to a structural model of why families decline the lottery, built on an expected-value calculation over the estimated $\theta_k$.

\subsection{The cost of application}
\label{sec:cost-of-application}
Our quality estimates $\theta_k$ measure the value of \emph{applying} to a school, not of attending it. A family that accepts automatic promotion attends its TK school with certainty; a family that enters the lottery attends its $k$-th choice only if every higher-ranked school rejects it. Valuing lottery participation therefore requires admission probabilities.

\paragraph{Expected value of the lottery.} A family that ranks schools by preference and is assigned the highest-ranked school that admits it has expected payoff
\[
\mathrm{EU} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \theta_k \, P_k \prod_{j \prec k} \left( 1 - P_j \right),
\]
where schools are indexed $1, \dots, K$ in decreasing order of $\theta$ (truthful preference order, given SFUSD's mechanism is strategy-proof), $j \prec k$ denotes every school ranked above $k$, and $P_k$ is school $k$'s admission probability. This is exactly $P(\text{admit}_1)\theta_1 + P(\text{admit}_2)P(\text{reject}_1)\theta_2 + \cdots$: a family only reaches its $k$-th choice if it was rejected everywhere it ranked higher. We treat rejection as independent across schools---a simplification forced by not observing individual-level match outcomes, which would let rejections correlate (e.g.\ through a common lottery number) in ways we cannot identify here.

$P_k$ itself we proxy with $\min(1, \text{capacity}_k / \text{demand}_k)$: $\text{demand}_k$ is school $k$'s average total (all-rank) lottery request volume over the estimation window, and $\text{capacity}_k$ is its actual kindergarten enrollment, averaged over the two most recent CDE school-level enrollment files (2023--24, 2024--25)---occupied seats being the closest available proxy for how many seats exist. Matching lottery school names to CDE school names (by hand where needed) gives a capacity for all \numSchools{} schools in the window, and admission probabilities from \numAdmitMin{} (\numAdmitMinSchool) and \numAdmitTop{} (\numTopSchool) to a median of \numAdmitMedian, with only Mission Education Center ES undersubscribed. (The full panel adds 15 standalone TK program sites that appear in early cycles' kindergarten listings, with no separately reported kindergarten enrollment; they are not in the main window, and in the full-panel robustness check they fall back to $P_k = 1$.) Code and the full crosswalk are in \texttt{src/capacity.py}.

Plugging in gives $\mathrm{EU} = \numEUbase$, below \numTopSchool's $\theta$ of $\numTopTheta$ (the best K school in the district by this measure), as it must be once admission is uncertain.

\paragraph{Forgone option value.} A family offered automatic promotion into school $k$ gives up $\mathrm{EU} - \theta_k$ by accepting. Averaged over the \numPromoted{} families who accepted (weighting each school by its acceptances), this is $\numFOVbase$ utility units, comparable to the $\theta$-gap between \numTopSchool{} and \numFOVbaseSchool{} (\#\numFOVbaseRank{} of \numSchools). Under the model of \S\ref{sec:promotion-rates} it is the lottery distaste of a family exactly indifferent between promotion and lottery; that section uses observed take-up to move from this threshold to an average.

Two schools---West Portal ES and Alvarado ES, the highest-$\theta$ promotion-eligible schools (\#2 and \#3 of \numSchools)---have \emph{negative} forgone option value: a family zoned there is better off taking the certain seat than gambling on the lottery, admission odds included, even with no distaste for the lottery at all. Families at these two schools who nonetheless entered the lottery were, by this model, taking a negative-expected-value bet (heterogeneous beliefs, an idiosyncratic preference draw we do not observe, or model misspecification are all possible). Figure~\ref{fig:quality-by-promotion-status} marks $\mathrm{EU}$ against the distribution of $\theta_k$ (left panel) and plots the measure by school (right panel).

This is still not the dollar-denominated answer the introduction wants, and the independence assumption above is a real one (true deferred-acceptance rejections likely correlate through priorities and lottery numbers in ways that would push $\mathrm{EU}$ further down, since correlated rejection reduces the value of a long list relative to independent draws). The next paragraph addresses a larger problem with the same formula.

\paragraph{A capacity-constrained simulation.} The formula above gives a single representative family an independent admission draw at every school, so that family collects a ticket at each of the most popular schools. Under it, a family lands in one of the top 8 schools with probability $\numReachTopEight$ and one of the top 15 with probability $\numReachTopFifteen$; but the top 8 schools have roughly \numSeatsTopEight{} kindergarten seats for roughly 4{,}000 applicants, so at most about \numSeatsTopEightShare{} of families can. Summed over families, the formula fills the best schools many times over, which is why only \numAboveEUbase{} of \numSchools{} schools have $\theta_k$ above the baseline $\mathrm{EU}$.

We therefore also simulate the 2025--26 lottery for the full cohort under the model of \S\ref{sec:model}, with seat limits. The cohort is the \numCohort{} families who would have applied absent the opt-out (lottery applicants plus the \numPromoted{} who accepted promotion). Each family draws $u_{ik} = \theta_k + \varepsilon_{ik}$ and an outside option $\xi + \varepsilon_{i0}$, and ranks every school it prefers to the outside option; $\xi$ is calibrated so the mean list length matches the \numReqPerApplicant{} requests per applicant the panel implies for the but-for cohort. Seats are each school's CDE kindergarten enrollment, \numSeats{} in total. Seats are assigned by random serial dictatorship---one lottery number per family, no priorities, since SFUSD's sibling, attendance-area, and CTIP1 priorities are unobserved without choice data---and families placed nowhere on their list are assigned administratively to a random remaining seat, as SFUSD does. In the simulation, \numSimPlaced{} of families are placed from their list, \numSimAdmin{} administratively, and \numSimNoSeat{} find no seat left (CDE enrollment counts occupied seats, so it understates true capacity).

We value the outcome by the $\theta$ of the assigned school, the common-value component, so the result is directly comparable to $\theta_k$ of the certain TK seat. This gives $\mathrm{EU}^{\text{cap}} = \numEUcap$, against $\numEUbase$ for the baseline formula. Because seats are scarce in aggregate, every seat is filled, and $\mathrm{EU}^{\text{cap}}$ is exactly the seat-weighted average of $\theta_k$ (it does not vary across simulation draws): in expectation, a family entering an oversubscribed lottery receives the average seat. Heterogeneous preferences determine \emph{who} gets which seat, but not the average $\theta$ of the seats handed out. They do matter for the full utility $\theta_k + \varepsilon_{ik}$ of the assignment, which we set aside here because the same idiosyncratic term attaches to the certain TK seat (see \S\ref{sec:promotion-rates}).

Against $\mathrm{EU}^{\text{cap}}$, \numAboveEUcap{} of \numSchools{} schools have $\theta_k$ above the lottery's expected value, and the family-weighted forgone option value falls to $\numFOVcap$ (about the $\theta$-gap between \numTopSchool{} and \numFOVcapSchool, \#\numFOVcapRank{} of \numSchools). Under the simulation, \numNegFOVcapSchools{} promotion-eligible schools, accounting for \numNegFOVcapFamilies{} of the \numPromoted{} acceptances, now have negative forgone option value: for those families, accepting promotion is the better bet even with no distaste for the lottery. Figure~\ref{fig:quality-by-promotion-status} shows both versions of $\mathrm{EU}$ and of the forgone option value. We take the capacity-constrained simulation as our preferred measure of the lottery's value, and report the baseline formula alongside it as an upper bound on that value. The baseline places a family in one of the top 8 schools with probability $\numReachTopEight$ when at most about \numSeatsTopEightShare{} of families can be, so it overstates what the lottery delivers and therefore overstates distaste. The simulation errs in the other direction: it ignores priorities, which concentrate popular seats among families with sibling or attendance-area claims, so a family without them does somewhat worse than the average seat. Both value the lottery over the full set of schools rather than over the list a family actually submits; because observed list lengths are variable and only coarsely reported beyond the ninth rank (\S\ref{sec:data}), recomputing $\mathrm{EU}$ over the top $n$ ranks for a range of $n$ is a natural robustness check we leave for the individual data.

Per-school results, admission probabilities, and the CDE-matching crosswalk are in \texttt{output/lottery\_distaste\_estimates.csv} and \texttt{output/school\_admission\_probabilities.csv}; \texttt{output/lottery\_distaste\_summary.csv} carries the summary statistics, and the estimation code lives in \texttt{run\_distaste\_analysis.py} and \texttt{src/}.


\section{Promotion rates}
\label{sec:promotion-rates}
Not every eligible family accepted promotion: \numPromoted{} of \numEligibleFamilies{} (\numTakeup) did. As described in \S\ref{sec:model}, the roster's two-program buildings (DeAvila, Huerta) are combined, giving \numEligibleBuildings{} buildings. The roster's eligibility count matches each school's 2024--25 TK enrollment in CDE's census-day file to within two students at \numCdeMatchWithinTwo{} of the \numEligibleBuildings{} schools, with the largest discrepancy \numCdeMatchMaxDiff{} students, so the school-level ratio $r_k = \text{accepted}_k / \text{eligible}_k$ is exactly the share of last year's TK class that took the guaranteed seat. Rates range from \numRateMin{} (Longfellow, Flynn, Chavez) to \numRateMax{} (Key); per-school detail is in \texttt{output/promotion\_rates.csv}.

\paragraph{Promotion rates and school quality.} If a guaranteed seat is worth more at a better school, promotion rates should rise with school quality. They do not (Figure~\ref{fig:promotion-rate}). Across the \numEligibleBuildings{} promotion-eligible buildings, the correlation between $r_k$ and our demand-based $\theta_k$ is $\numCorrRateTheta$; with SBAC 2023--24 percent met-or-exceeded it is $\numCorrRateELA$ (ELA) and $\numCorrRateMath$ (math), none distinguishable from zero. The only covariate with a hint of a relationship is the school's unduplicated-pupil (low-income/EL/foster) share, at $\numCorrRateUnduplicated$ ($p \approx \numCorrRateUnduplicatedP$). Since $\theta_k$, test scores, and demographics are themselves highly collinear across these schools (pairwise $|\rho|$ from $\numRhoMin$ to $\numRhoMax$), there is no room to say more with \numEligibleBuildings{} observations.

\paragraph{Using the rate to estimate distaste.} The probability-weighted measure of \S\ref{sec:cost-of-application}, $\mathrm{EU} - \theta_k$, is the option value a family forgoes by accepting promotion. It does not use the promotion rate at all: it is computed identically whether 10\% or 100\% of a school's TK families accepted. Under the model, it is the lottery distaste of the \emph{marginal} family, the one indifferent between promotion and lottery. Inverting the acceptance probability of the model section gives, school by school,
\[
c_k = \underbrace{\ln \frac{r_k}{1 - r_k}}_{\text{logit of promotion rate}} + \underbrace{\left( \mathrm{EU} - \theta_k \right)}_{\text{forgone option value}} .
\]
A promotion rate below one does mean families find the lottery less distasteful than they would if everyone had accepted (as $r_k \to 1$, $c_k \to \infty$), but the relevant comparison is with the option-value measure, which implicitly sets $r_k = 1/2$. Since \numTakeup{} of families accepted, the typical family's distaste is \emph{above} the marginal family's, and incorporating the rate raises the estimate. Fitting a single $c$ by grouped-logit maximum likelihood across all \numEligibleFamilies{} eligible families, against the preferred capacity-constrained $\mathrm{EU}^{\text{cap}}$, gives $\hat{c} = \numChatCap$ (s.e.\ $\numChatCapSE$, which ignores sampling error in $\theta_k$ and $\mathrm{EU}$ and is therefore too small), against $\numFOVcap$ for the forgone option value. On the scale of \S\ref{sec:cost-of-application}, $\numChatCap$ is roughly the $\theta$-gap between \numTopSchool{} and \numChatCapSchool{} (\#\numChatCapRank{} of \numSchools), close to the gap between the best and the median school in the district. Using the baseline $\mathrm{EU}$ instead gives the larger $\hat{c} = \numChat$ (s.e.\ $\numChatSE$; against a forgone option value of $\numFOVbase$), roughly the gap between \numTopSchool{} and \numChatSchool{} (\#\numChatRank). With the slope restricted to one, the data identify only $c - \mathrm{EU}$, so the two estimates differ by exactly the gap between the two expected values, $\numEUgap$, and trace the same acceptance curve; the choice between them is a choice of $\mathrm{EU}$, not something the promotion data can settle.

\paragraph{The slope restriction.} The model ties the slope of acceptance in $\theta_k$ to one: a family at a school one utility unit better should be $\Lambda'$ more likely to accept. Freeing that coefficient gives $\hat\beta = \numBetaFree$ (s.e.\ $\numBetaFreeSE$); the restriction $\beta = 1$ is firmly rejected. The data indicate that promotion decisions are close to unrelated to how much the city wants to attend the school in question. Three readings, which are not mutually exclusive:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Selection on match value.} Families at a low-$\theta$ school who chose it for TK value it more than the city does ($z_{ik}$ and $x_i'\beta_k$ are doing the work); this is exactly what individual data would let us test.
    \item \emph{Learning.} A year at the school replaces the citywide prior $\theta_k$ with first-hand information, so $\theta_k$ is the wrong index for TK families.
    \item \emph{Non-acceptance is not lottery entry.} Some non-accepting families leave SFUSD (private, charter, out of city) rather than entering the lottery. Those are rejections of the school, not a taste for the lottery, and they bias $\hat{c}$ \emph{down}.
\end{itemize}
Under any of these, $\hat{c}$ ($\numChatCap$, or $\numChat$ under the baseline $\mathrm{EU}$) is best read as the average certainty premium implied by a \numTakeup{} take-up rate, rather than a structural estimate validated by cross-school variation. The directions matter as much as the readings. Every channel above except the last pushes $\hat{c}$ \emph{up}: both selection on match value and learning make a family's own school worth more to that family than $\theta_k$ says, so part of what we attribute to distaste for the lottery is in fact taste for the school. Only families who leave SFUSD rather than enter the lottery push the other way, and that channel is the one of the three that could be bounded with data we have asked for: October enrollment would show how many of the \numNonAccepting{} non-accepting families never enrolled in an SFUSD kindergarten. On balance we therefore read $\hat{c}$ as an upper bound on distaste for lottery participation, subject to that one offset. The individual data are what would separate these channels.

\section{Estimating risk aversion directly}
\label{sec:risk-aversion}
The certainty premium $c$ of \S\ref{sec:promotion-rates} is a reduced-form ``distaste'' added to a risk-neutral comparison. An alternative reading of the same choices is that families are risk-averse over school quality: they prefer the certain seat because the lottery is risky, not because lottery participation is costly in itself. This appendix estimates that risk aversion directly and compares it to canonical values.

\paragraph{Setup.} Each of the \numEligibleFamilies{} eligible families makes one binary choice between a sure outcome, kindergarten at its own TK school ($s_i = \theta_{k(i)}$), and a lottery $L$ over the $\theta$ of the school the kindergarten lottery would assign it. Without individual data we treat these as \numEligibleFamilies{} choices by a single representative agent who balances the guaranteed payoff of staying against the uncertain lottery. All families face the same lottery, and the sure outcome varies across the \numEligibleBuildings{} buildings, giving \numEligibleBuildings{} choice ``designs.'' We use both lottery definitions from \S\ref{sec:cost-of-application}: the baseline, in which school $k$ is reached with probability $P_k \prod_{j \prec k}(1 - P_j)$, and the capacity-constrained simulation, in which $L$ is the distribution of assigned schools among families who receive a seat (the \numSimNoSeat{} with no seat are excluded, which if anything understates the lottery's downside).

We use constant absolute risk aversion (CARA), $u(x; r) = (1 - e^{-r x}) / r$, with $r$ measured per unit of $\theta$. CRRA is unsuitable here: $\theta$ takes negative values and has no natural zero (each specification normalizes it differently), whereas CARA is invariant to adding a constant to every outcome, so $r$ does not depend on the normalization. $\theta$'s scale is pinned by the unit-scale logit shocks, so $r$ is well defined. Code is in \texttt{src/risk\_aversion.py}.

\paragraph{Representative agent.} Following the noise-in-certainty-equivalent specification (which, unlike utility-space noise, keeps choice probabilities monotone in $r$; Apesteguia and Ballester 2018), the family chooses the lottery with probability
\[
\Pr(\text{lottery} \mid k) = \Lambda\left( \frac{\mathrm{CE}_L(r) - \theta_k}{\mu} \right), \qquad \mathrm{CE}_L(r) = -\frac{1}{r} \ln \mathbb{E}_L\left[ e^{-r \theta} \right].
\]
Setting $\mu = 1$, the scale of the logit shocks that already normalizes $\theta$ in the promotion model, this is exactly the acceptance model of \S\ref{sec:promotion-rates} with the certainty premium replaced by curvature: $\mathrm{CE}_L(\hat r) = \mathrm{EU} - \hat c = \numCE$ under either lottery definition. The estimates are $\hat r = \numRbase$ (robust s.e.\ $\numRbaseSE$) for the baseline lottery and $\hat r = \numRcap$ (robust s.e.\ $\numRcapSE$) for the capacity-constrained lottery (Figure~\ref{fig:risk-aversion}, left); the implied risk premia, $\mathbb{E}[L] - \mathrm{CE}_L$, equal $\hat c$ ($\numChat$ and $\numChatCap$). Like $\hat c$, these standard errors ignore estimation error in $\theta$ and the admission probabilities. With these data, a risk-neutral family with distaste $c$ and a CARA family with $r = \hat r$ make identical choices: both explain the same single moment, the \numTakeup{} take-up rate. Individual data would help separate them, because families with different priorities face lotteries with different spreads, and risk aversion responds to the spread while a fixed distaste does not. The separation is not automatic, however: priority is a function of where a family lives and whether it has a sibling enrolled, and those same variables plausibly shift the utility of the school itself, so identification would rest on an exclusion restriction---some determinant of the spread a family faces that does not enter its preferences directly---which we do not have and would need to argue for.

\paragraph{What the data cannot identify.} Freeing $\mu$ fails: the likelihood improves without limit toward $r \to \infty$, and the guard in the code reports non-identification. This is the flat promotion-rate gradient of \S\ref{sec:promotion-rates} again: the slope of acceptance in $\theta_k$ is $1/\mu$, and it is indistinguishable from zero. The utility-space (Fechner) and range-normalized (contextual; Wilcox 2011) noise specifications likewise run to a parameter bound. A heterogeneous-agent model, in which each family has its own $r_i \sim N(m, \sigma^2)$ and chooses the lottery exactly when $r_i$ is below its school's indifference point $r^*_k$ (where $\mathrm{CE}_L(r^*_k) = \theta_k$), implies $\Pr(\text{lottery} \mid k) = \Phi((r^*_k - m)/\sigma)$, a probit of choice on $r^*_k$. The probit is estimated over school-level designs rather than the \numEligibleFamilies{} families directly: \numRiskDesignsBase{} designs for the baseline lottery, and \numRiskDesignsCap{} for the capacity-constrained one, where \numRiskDroppedCap{} families at one school are dropped because their design is dominated: that school's own $\theta_k$ lies at or below the worst outcome the capacity-constrained lottery can deliver, so the lottery weakly dominates the certain seat for \emph{every} $r_i$ and the choice carries no information about $m$ or $\sigma$. The share choosing the lottery should therefore rise with $r^*_k$; it is flat (Figure~\ref{fig:risk-aversion}, right; probit slope $\numProbitSlopeBase$, s.e.\ $\numProbitSlopeBaseSE$, for the baseline lottery and $\numProbitSlopeCap$, s.e.\ $\numProbitSlopeCapSE$, for the capacity-constrained one). Taken literally, the 95\% confidence bound on the slope requires a dispersion of risk aversion $\sigma$ of at least $\numSigmaBoundBase$ (baseline) or $\numSigmaBoundCap$ (capacity-constrained) per $\theta$ unit, an order of magnitude beyond the representative estimate; we read this as a rejection of the pure-heterogeneity model rather than as an estimate. With one choice per family, within-family noise and between-family heterogeneity cannot be separated: the representative-agent model attributes all variation to noise, and the heterogeneous model attributes all of it to heterogeneity.

\paragraph{Fragility in the tail.} Matching a certainty equivalent of $\numCE$ requires the lottery's worst outcomes to carry nearly all the weight. Under the baseline lottery, the probability of being assigned a school with $\theta$ below $\numCE$ is about $\numProbBelowCEbase$; under the capacity-constrained lottery it is \numProbBelowCEcap. Accordingly, $\hat r$ is driven by the lowest few schools: dropping the single lowest outcome and renormalizing moves $\hat r$ from $\numRbase$ to $\numRdropOneBase$ (baseline) and from $\numRcap$ to $\numRdropOneCap$ (capacity-constrained); dropping the lowest three moves both above $100$. Expected-utility curvature can rationalize \numTakeup{} take-up only by letting a few-percent (or vanishing) chance of the district's least-demanded schools dominate the decision. That pattern is better described by overweighting of small probabilities or worst-case reasoning (ambiguity aversion) than by smooth risk aversion, and is consistent with the probability distortions found in field insurance choices (Barseghyan et al.\ 2013).

\paragraph{Comparison to canonical risk aversion.} Canonical estimates are of relative risk aversion over money, so a comparison needs either a unit-free summary or a conversion from $\theta$ to dollars. We report both (Table~\ref{tab:canonical}).
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Unit-free.} The risk premium as a share of its maximum, $(\mathbb{E}[L] - \mathrm{CE}_L) / (\mathbb{E}[L] - \min L)$, is $\numShareMaxBase$ (baseline) and $\numShareMaxCap$ (capacity-constrained). On Holt and Laury's (2002) high-risk lottery at the 50/50 row ($\$3.85$ or $\$0.10$), the CRRA coefficients producing the same shares are $\numHoltLauryBase$ (baseline) and $\numHoltLauryCap$ (capacity-constrained). The baseline figure sits inside the $0.3$--$0.7$ range typical of small-stakes laboratory choices, while the preferred capacity-constrained figure sits above it (Holt and Laury 2002; Harrison and Rutstr\"om 2008). Measured against the lottery's own range, then, families are about as risk-averse as lab subjects---but the share depends on $\min L$, which is precisely the fragile tail above, so we do not read this agreement as support for the CARA reading.
    \item \emph{In dollars.} If one unit of $\theta$ is worth $D$ dollars and the relevant wealth is $W$, relative risk aversion is $\rho = (\hat r / D) W$. Taking $W = \$1$ million as an illustrative San Francisco family's wealth, and $D = \$50{,}000$ (one $\theta$ unit is roughly the gap between the district's most-demanded school and its \numOneUnitRank{}th), gives $\rho \approx \numCRRAfiftyBase$ (baseline) to $\numCRRAfiftyCap$ (capacity-constrained). For $\rho$ to fall to $2$, the upper end of the macro and labor-supply range (Chetty 2006), a single $\theta$ unit would have to be worth \numBreakevenBase--\numBreakevenCap. These magnitudes resemble the implausibly high coefficients implied by insurance deductible choices (Sydnor 2010), and Rabin's (2000) calibration argument applies: no reasonable curvature over lifetime wealth produces premia this large on stakes this size. We do not yet have a $\theta$-to-dollar conversion of our own; the distance coefficient from the individual data will supply one.
\end{itemize}
